# Circula business model options

**Status:** Exploration; no model selected  
**Owner:** Circula project owner  
**Last updated:** 2026-10-20  
**Scope:** Commercial paths that could capture value from Circula software, services, and adoption  
**Dependencies:** Product vision, deployment and operations, governance, and future brainstorming specifications  
**Open questions:** License, company structure, target buyer, hosting model, pricing, and jurisdiction-specific review.

## Business thesis

Circula’s commercial opportunity is to make community mutual-credit networks easier to start, operate, govern, and connect. If adoption grows, value can accrue to the creator through dependable software, hosting, implementation expertise, integrations, and a trusted ecosystem around the protocol.

A useful starting thesis is **open core, paid operations**: keep the essential accounting behavior inspectable and portable, then charge for services that save time or provide operational confidence. Communities should be able to leave with their data. Revenue should come from serving networks, not taking speculative positions in their credits or making the system depend on forced conversion to national currency.

This is a hypothesis to test with prospective networks, not a forecast or commitment.

## Value created and who may pay

| Customer or payer | Value Circula could provide |
|---|---|
| Small community network | Fast setup, accessible member tools, simple support, reliable backups |
| Cooperative or larger association | Member administration, permissions, reporting, imports, audit and governance workflows |
| Network operator | Managed hosting, upgrades, monitoring, recovery, migration, and incident support |
| Federation or network alliance | Directory, protocol support, inter-network coordination, and clearing operations if later governed |
| Developers and implementation partners | Stable APIs, documentation, compatibility testing, training, and paid support |

Members who only participate in ordinary exchanges should not need to pay a fee on every mutual-credit transfer in order for the software company to make money.

## Revenue models to evaluate

### 1. Hosted network subscriptions

Offer managed hosting for groups that prefer not to run infrastructure. Possible tiers could vary by network size, storage, support response, backup retention, or administrative tooling. A free or low-cost pilot tier can lower adoption barriers; higher tiers can fund reliability and support.

**Tradeoffs:** recurring revenue and simple onboarding, but creates a central service operator and obligations around privacy, uptime, data portability, and support. Self-hosting and exports remain important exit paths.

### 2. Managed self-hosted deployments

Charge for installation, upgrades, monitoring, backup configuration, recovery drills, and operational support while the customer controls its hosting environment. This fits organizations that require more control but lack staff time.

**Tradeoffs:** service revenue without requiring Circula to own every deployment; support complexity can grow across diverse customer environments.

### 3. Paid organization and administration features

Potential paid modules include richer role management, multi-site administration, branded member portals, scheduled reports, bulk import/export assistance, advanced audit views, and policy-change workflows. Core ledger correctness, member access to their own balances, and data export should not be held hostage to a paid tier.

**Tradeoffs:** clear upgrade path, but a fragmented product can weaken trust. Keep the free/community edition useful and document exactly what paid features add.

### 4. Onboarding and network design services

Provide paid setup packages covering configuration, member onboarding materials, migration from spreadsheets, governance workflow facilitation, and staff training. Templates can be free while tailored implementation and facilitation are paid.

**Tradeoffs:** useful early revenue and direct learning from customers; service delivery is labor-intensive and harder to scale.

### 5. Support contracts and reliability services

Sell support response plans, security and upgrade assistance, backup verification, recovery exercises, and operational reviews. Publish scope and service limits instead of implying a guarantee that no outage or loss can occur.

**Tradeoffs:** recurring revenue tied to customer confidence; requires sustainable operational capacity and clear responsibilities.

### 6. Integration and API services

Offer paid implementation of connectors for accounting exports, member-management systems, cooperative tools, or reporting systems. Keep public protocol documentation and ordinary data export open where feasible; charge for custom engineering, hosted interfaces, and support.

**Tradeoffs:** expands usefulness and adoption but can create maintenance burdens and expose new data flows that need careful permission design.

### 7. Federation and clearing support

If independent networks later choose to interoperate, Circula could offer a hosted directory, compatibility testing, federation operations, or clearing coordination. A network alliance could pay a subscription for these services.

**Tradeoffs:** potentially strong network effects and defensibility, but inter-network obligations, governance, defaults, and exits are high-stakes. Avoid a model that makes the company the unaccountable issuer or guarantor of network credits.

### 8. Training, certification, and partner ecosystem

Offer administrator training, implementation-partner certification, and compatibility testing for independent hosting providers. Certification should verify transparent criteria, not buy favorable placement or imply that a network is financially safe.

**Tradeoffs:** scales expertise beyond the core team, but requires credible standards and ongoing maintenance.

### 9. Sponsorships, grants, and institutional partnerships

Accept clearly disclosed support from foundations, cooperatives, universities, community-economy programs, or aligned institutions. Sponsorship can fund open protocol work, accessibility, translations, security reviews, or pilot deployments.

**Tradeoffs:** supports public-benefit development without charging members directly; funding can be episodic and may influence priorities unless governance and disclosure are clear.

### 10. Trademark, hosted brand, and commercial licensing

A trademark and documented compatibility program could let others build with Circula while preserving a clear identity for the official hosted service. Commercial licensing or dual licensing could be explored if customer demand requires it.

**Tradeoffs:** can distinguish official services and create licensing revenue; licensing choices affect contributors, adoption, and trust. Decide only after reviewing open-source goals, contributor rights, and professional legal advice.

### 11. Transaction fees: proceed cautiously

A fee on each credit transfer could appear to monetize growth directly, but it risks discouraging exchange, complicating the accounting model, and making the company dependent on network activity. It should not be the default business model. If ever considered, it must be separately disclosed, approved by the network, clearly denominated, and reviewed for accounting and jurisdiction-specific implications. Avoid hidden deductions from member balances.

## Recommended sequence to test

1. Interview prospective networks about their biggest operating costs and willingness to pay.
2. Run a small pilot and measure setup time, support load, member retention, and operational cost.
3. Test paid onboarding or managed hosting first; these have clear customer value without changing ledger rules.
4. Add paid administration and support options only after the core product and responsibilities are stable.
5. Consider integrations, certification, and federation services after repeated customer demand.
6. Reassess licensing and company structure before external contributions or commercial deployment make those choices costly to change.

## Value capture and trust principles

- Charge for convenience, reliability, expertise, and optional organizational capabilities.
- Keep mutual-credit balances separate from company revenue and operating funds.
- Do not sell member transaction histories or use them for advertising.
- Do not make export, basic ledger access, or fair exit contingent on payment.
- Avoid speculative token appreciation, pay-to-rank listings, or hidden transaction deductions.
- Make pricing, service limits, data access, and shutdown/export procedures visible before purchase.
- Keep platform operation separate from network governance and dispute decisions.
- Offer a route for self-hosted communities and independent implementations.

These principles aim to let the business profit if Circula becomes useful at scale while preserving the voluntary, community-directed qualities that make the project disruptive.

## Business questions for project-owner review

1. Should Circula be a for-profit company, a cooperative, a nonprofit with a trading subsidiary, or another structure?
2. Which software license best balances open adoption and sustainable commercial services?
3. Should the official hosted service be the main product, or one option alongside self-hosting?
4. What must always remain free and portable?
5. Which customer segment should fund the first sustainable revenue?
6. What data must the operator never monetize or repurpose?
7. What legal, privacy, tax, and accounting questions need qualified advice before paid launch?
